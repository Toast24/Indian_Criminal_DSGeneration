## 3 Methodology

### 3.1 Overview

We audit large language models acting as bail judges with a **paired counterfactual design**. Each real Indian bail case is presented to the model several times, and the prompts are identical except for one inserted line stating a demographic attribute of the accused (gender or caste). Because the case text is already demographically neutral, that line is the only demographic signal, so each case serves as its own control.

Figure 1 shows the end-to-end pipeline. Anonymised case facts go in. They are filtered, stratified by whether the attribute is legally relevant to bail, and expanded into counterfactual prompts. The model returns a structured decision (grant or deny, a short reasoning and the statutes relied on). These decisions are tested for bias against a same-prompt noise floor. In a final stage, the model is asked to explain its own decision changes, so that we can test whether its self-explanations are faithful and whether it recognises the law where the attribute does matter.

> **Figure 1: Overview of the counterfactual bail-audit pipeline.** The pipeline has three stages.
> 1. **Data and counterfactuals (blue):** 1,200 anonymised bail cases are filtered for outcome leakage, stratified into *legally irrelevant* and *legally relevant* strata, sampled with a fixed seed, and expanded into prompts that differ only in a `Gender of accused` or `Caste of accused` line.
> 2. **Decision elicitation and bias tests (green):** each prompt is sent independently to the LLM judge (sarvam-105b or gpt-oss-120b). The structured decisions are compared within each case using paired tests, and every decision flip is benchmarked against a noise floor from re-running the *same* prompt.
> 3. **Reasoning probes (orange):** cases whose decision flipped (*real flips*), cases whose decision changed when the same prompt was simply re-run (*placebo flips*, shown with fake attribute labels) and legally relevant cases that did *not* flip are returned to the same model, which is asked to explain itself. A fresh-context model call classifies each explanation.
>
> The key novelty is in stage 3. The placebo-flip control tests whether a model's explanation tracks the true cause of its behaviour, and the relevant-case probe tests whether apparent fairness reflects correct application of the law or blindness to it.

### 3.2 Data Collection and Preprocessing

**Source.** We use a corpus of 1,200 Indian bail applications (906 regular, 268 anticipatory, 19 interim, 7 other). Each record has a summary of the facts and arguments (median 65 words), the bail type, the accused's real gender and the real court outcome (736 granted, 464 rejected). The facts were anonymised and rewritten to be gender-neutral in advance ("the accused", singular *they*), so the only demographic signal the model receives is the line we insert. The real gender is never shown to the model.

**Leakage filter.** Facts that reveal the outcome (e.g. "bail was granted", "enlarged on bail", "petition dismissed") would let the model copy the answer. We removed them with word-bounded regular expressions.

**Stratification by legal relevance.** For each attribute we split cases by whether Indian law permits the attribute to affect bail.
- **Gender, legally relevant:** grave offences (murder, attempt to murder, dowry death, kidnapping for ransom, dacoity, commercial-quantity NDPS offences, terrorism; IPC/BNS sections matched explicitly). Here the proviso to CrPC s.437(1) / BNSS s.480(1) allows courts to grant bail to a woman even for offences punishable by death or life imprisonment.
- **Gender, legally irrelevant:** all other offences.
- **Gender exclusions:** sex offences (rape, POCSO, IPC s.354/376/509 and BNS equivalents), because swapping the accused's gender changes the offence itself.
- **Caste, legally relevant:** every case that invokes the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act or caste. In all of these, caste refers to the *victim*, and the accused is charged under the Act. That implies a non-SC/ST accused, because the Act penalises offences *by* non-SC/ST persons *against* SC/ST persons; s.18 also bars anticipatory bail. An SC label on the accused therefore makes the Atrocities Act charge legally doubtful.
- **Caste, legally irrelevant:** cases with no caste content.
- **Caste exclusions:** none for sex offences, because a caste swap does not alter the offence.

**Sampling.** After filtering, 1,032 cases are eligible for the gender study.
- **Gender sample:** 150 legally-irrelevant and 150 legally-relevant cases, stratified by real outcome (seed 42). A 50-case pilot is excluded to keep the confirmatory sample clean.
- **Caste sample:** the same legally-irrelevant cases (149, after one case that mentions caste is moved), so the gender and caste results are directly comparable, plus all 10 caste-relevant cases in the corpus.

The design choice throughout is to hold the case set fixed and vary only the attribute. This trades breadth for a clean within-case comparison.

### 3.3 Core Approach: Paired Counterfactual Prompting

For case $x_i$ and attribute value $a$ (gender: *Male, Female*; caste: *none, General (Brahmin), OBC (Yadav), SC (Dalit/Jatav)*), the model returns

$$\hat{y}_{i,a} = f_\theta\big(x_i \oplus \ell_a;\ P\big) \in \{\text{grant}, \text{deny}\}, \tag{1}$$

where $\ell_a$ is the inserted attribute line, $P$ is a fixed system prompt that instructs the model to act as an Indian criminal court judge deciding only on the legal merits, and $f_\theta$ is the LLM. The caste design includes a **no-caste arm** ($\ell_{\text{none}} = \varnothing$), which is the demographically neutral baseline.

The bias estimate for arms $a$ and $b$ is the paired difference in grant rate

$$\Delta_{a,b} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \big(\mathbb{1}[\hat{y}_{i,b}=\text{grant}] - \mathbb{1}[\hat{y}_{i,a}=\text{grant}]\big), \tag{2}$$

and the flip rate is $\varphi_{\text{between}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \mathbb{1}[\hat{y}_{i,a} \neq \hat{y}_{i,b}]$.

Because LLM decoding is not fully deterministic even at temperature 0, a flip is only evidence of attribute sensitivity if it is more frequent than the **same-prompt flip rate**

$$\varphi_{\text{within}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \mathbb{1}\big[\hat{y}^{(1)}_{i,a} \neq \hat{y}^{(2)}_{i,a}\big]. \tag{3}$$

Here $\hat{y}^{(1)}$ and $\hat{y}^{(2)}$ are two independent runs of the identical prompt. This directly addresses a gap in prior counterfactual-fairness audits, which usually compare outcome rates across groups without checking whether the differences exceed the model's own run-to-run variation.

#### 3.3.1 Counterfactual Prompt Construction

The user prompt contains, in order:
- the case ID
- the attribute line (or nothing, for the no-caste arm)
- the bail type
- any statutory sections extracted from the facts by regular expression
- the facts and arguments
- the instruction "Decide whether bail should be granted. Return JSON only."

The system prompt asks the model to weigh gravity, evidence, risk of absconding or tampering, custody already served and applicable law. It also asks for a JSON object with `case_id`, `bail_granted` (boolean), `reasoning` (under 75 words) and `relevant_acts` (at most 4 statutes).

Each case's arms are sent as **separate, stateless calls** in a randomised order, so the model never sees two versions of the same case and order effects are spread across arms.

#### 3.3.2 Inference Settings

Both models are queried with reasoning effort *low*, JSON-object output and temperature 0 for the primary run. The noise floor uses two complementary measurements:
1. a second temperature-0 run of the reference arm (*no caste* or *Male*) on every case, and
2. three temperature-0.7 repeats per arm on a subset (60 caste cases; 100 gender cases for gpt-oss), comparing within-arm and between-arm disagreement.

Responses are validated: the JSON must parse, `bail_granted` must be boolean and `case_id` must match. Invalid or truncated responses are retried up to three times. Calls that still fail are logged as failures and never replaced with a default decision.

### 3.4 Reasoning Probes and Post-Processing

The final stage asks *why* the model behaved as it did and whether its account can be trusted. Each probe is a fresh, single-turn call to **the same model** that made the decisions. The model sees the case facts and its own earlier decisions, each with its original reasoning, and every free-text answer is capped at 500 characters.

1. **"Why did you flip?", on irrelevant and relevant flips.**
   - *Real flips* are cases whose primary decision differed across arms.
   - *Placebo flips* are cases where the **same prompt** gave different decisions on reruns. These decisions are shown to the model with randomly assigned fake attribute labels, so the true cause is pure noise. A faithful model should blame the attribute for real flips more often than for placebo flips.
   - **Neutral framing:** "Below are N decisions you made on versions of this bail application… The decisions are not all the same. What explains the difference?" The model picks the cause from a fixed list: differences between inputs, the attribute, different weighing of the same facts, randomness or inconsistency, or other.
   - **Leading framing:** the prompt states that the inputs were identical except for the attribute line and asks why the decision changed. A second turn then challenges the model: "only the attribute changed… was your decision influenced by it? Be direct." Sarvam's API returns empty output for multi-turn histories, so the first answer is quoted inside the challenge prompt.
2. **"Why didn't you flip?", on legally relevant cases with identical decisions.**
   - Gender: up to 30 sampled cases. Caste: all such cases.
   - The model is asked whether the attribute *should* have made a legal difference, under which provision, and which version the law treats more leniently.

**Classifying the answers.** A separate fresh-context call to the same model labels each explanation:
- attribution: attribute, legal factors, inconsistency/error, or other
- whether it implies the facts differed
- in the challenge turn, whether it acknowledges, partially acknowledges or denies bias, and whether it *doubles down* by defending the differing decisions

Statute citations are also checked with regular expressions for the specific provision (the women's proviso to s.437/480; SC/ST Act applicability).

### 3.5 Design Choices and Justification

- **Explicit attribute line rather than names or dialect.** An explicit label isolates the attribute with no lexical confounds. Caste and gender proxies such as surnames carry regional and religious signals as well. The cost is that explicit labels may *under*-estimate bias that operates through implicit cues. We note this as a limitation.
- **Within-case pairing.** Every case is its own control, which removes case-mix confounding and lets us use exact paired tests (McNemar, Cochran's Q). This needs far fewer cases than comparing outcomes between groups of different cases.
- **A same-prompt noise floor.** At temperature 0, sarvam-105b disagreed with itself on 10–12% of reruns of the identical prompt. Without this floor, flip counts would be mistaken for bias.
- **Legal-relevance strata.** Equal treatment is not always correct. The strata separate *unjustified* sensitivity (any effect in irrelevant cases) from *legally required* sensitivity (the women's proviso; SC/ST Act applicability), so the audit tests both fairness and legal fidelity.
- **Placebo flips for probing faithfulness.** Asking a model "were you biased?" mostly measures sycophancy. Comparing its blame rate for real flips against its blame rate for flips known to be noise turns self-explanation into a testable claim.
- **Two models.** sarvam-105b is an India-focused model, so it is the natural candidate for Indian legal reasoning. gpt-oss-120b is a widely deployed open-weight reasoning model. Both are run with identical prompts and the same low reasoning effort.

### 3.8 Evaluation Metrics

**Table 3: Evaluation metrics.**

| Metric | Definition | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Grant rate per arm | Share of cases granted, with 95% Wilson CI | Descriptive outcome level |
| Paired difference $\Delta_{a,b}$ (Eq. 2) | Difference in grant rate between arms on the same cases; 95% case-bootstrap CI (2,000 resamples) | **Primary effect size** |
| Exact McNemar test | Two-sided binomial test on discordant pairs ($a$-only vs $b$-only grants) | **Primary significance test** for pairwise contrasts |
| Cochran's Q | Omnibus test across the 4 caste arms | Is any caste arm different? |
| Pooled "any label vs none" | Mean of the 3 caste arms minus the no-caste arm per case; sign-flip permutation test (10,000 permutations) | Does mentioning caste at all matter? |
| Flips vs noise | $\varphi_{\text{between}}$ vs $\varphi_{\text{within}}$ (Eq. 3), paired sign-flip permutation test | Is attribute sensitivity above the model's own run-to-run variation? |
| Attribute mention asymmetry | Share of decision reasonings that mention the attribute, per arm; exact McNemar | Does the model *talk* about the attribute differently for different groups? |
| Legal-provision citation | Share citing the women's proviso to s.437/480, or the SC/ST Act | Legal awareness |
| Accuracy vs real outcome | Agreement with the actual court decision | Sanity check (not a fairness metric) |
| Blame rate, real vs placebo | Share of explanations attributing the flip to the attribute; Fisher's exact test | **Primary faithfulness metric** |
| Acknowledges / doubles down | Challenge-turn stance, from self-report and the model judge | Sycophancy and robustness of self-explanation |
| "Should differ" / correct direction | In relevant no-flip cases: share saying the attribute should matter, and share naming the legally favoured version (Female; SC) | Legal fidelity where equal treatment is *not* required |

**Significance.** We use α = 0.05 with the Holm–Bonferroni correction applied to all tests **within each run** (17–21 tests for caste, 5–6 for gender).

**Primary metrics.** For the bias question, the paired difference and McNemar test in the *legally irrelevant* stratum are primary, because any effect there is unjustified. For the reasoning question, the gap in blame rate between real and placebo flips is primary.

**Limitations of the metrics.**
- **Accuracy against real outcomes** is reported only as a sanity check. Court outcomes are not a neutral gold standard.
- **Mention and citation metrics** are keyword-based. Our citation check counts any mention of s.437/480 near "woman" or "proviso", and any mention of the SC/ST Act. Manual reading shows several hits are statements that the Act does *not* make caste relevant, so citation rates are **upper bounds**. "Names the legally favoured version" is the stricter measure.
- **The model judge** is the same model as the one being audited. Its labels agreed with the models' own self-reported causes in almost every case. Human annotation of a subset would strengthen this.
- **The caste-relevant stratum** has only 10 cases and is reported as exploratory.

### 3.9 Baselines

Our baselines are the court cases themselves in demographically neutral form, plus two variation controls and the real outcomes.

**Table 4: Baselines and controls.**

| Baseline / control | What it is | Claim it tests |
|---|---|---|
| **Neutral case text** | The anonymised, gender-neutral case as written, which the model sees in every arm | Holds all case content fixed, so differences between arms can only come from the inserted line |
| **No-caste arm** | The same prompt with no caste line at all | Whether *any* caste label, regardless of group, changes decisions (caste salience) |
| **Reference arm across groups** (Male for gender; General for caste) | Paired comparison of each group against the dominant group | Classic counterfactual-fairness contrasts: Female vs Male, SC vs General, OBC vs General |
| **Same-prompt rerun (noise floor)** | The identical prompt sent again (T=0) and three T=0.7 repeats | Is attribute sensitivity larger than the model's own run-to-run variation? |
| **Placebo flips** | Decision changes caused only by re-running, shown with fake attribute labels | Do self-explanations track the real cause or just the label they are shown? |
| **Real court outcomes** | The actual decision in each case | Whether the model is a reasonable bail decision-maker at all (accuracy), independent of fairness |
| **Second model** | gpt-oss-120b vs sarvam-105b on identical prompts and cases | Whether the findings are model-specific |

The neutral text and the no-caste arm isolate the effect of the label. The noise floor separates attribute sensitivity from randomness. The placebo isolates whether an explanation is faithful or merely agrees with the framing. The model comparison tests whether the findings generalise.

### 3.10 Implementation Details

All experiments use hosted inference APIs; no model is trained or fine-tuned.
- **sarvam-105b:** called through the Sarvam AI API (`sarvamai` SDK 0.1.36).
- **gpt-oss-120b, gender decisions:** called directly on Groq.
- **gpt-oss-120b, everything else:** called through OpenRouter, **pinned to the Groq provider with fallbacks disabled**, so that the serving stack matches across runs.
- **Shared settings:** reasoning effort low, JSON-object response format, temperature 0 (primary) and 0.7 (noise repeats).
- **Output-token cap:** sarvam-105b uses 3,000–7,000 hidden reasoning tokens per decision (mean ≈ 4,400) and returned empty answers at the default 2,048-token limit, so we set `max_tokens` to 12,000 (raised to 30,000 for three probe calls). gpt-oss-120b uses about 150 output tokens per decision.
- **Reasoning traces:** Sarvam's API does not return its reasoning trace. For gpt-oss-120b we log the trace returned by OpenRouter.
- **Seeds and sampling:** case sampling, arm order and placebo label assignment use seed 42.
- **Concurrency and logging:** calls run with 4 (Sarvam) or 8 (OpenRouter) parallel workers. Results are appended to a resumable JSONL log after every call. Retries use exponential back-off. A hard budget stop prevents overspending.
- **Statistics:** computed with SciPy and statsmodels.

## 4 Results and Discussion

### 4.1 Experiment 1: Main Results

**Objective.** Do sarvam-105b and gpt-oss-120b change bail decisions when only the accused's stated gender or caste changes? If so, is the change larger than the models' own run-to-run noise? And do they treat the attribute differently where the law says they should?

**Findings.**

1. **No unjustified bias in decisions.** In none of the four runs does the accused's gender or caste significantly change grant rates after correction.
   - **Main contrasts:** the 95% CIs for Female−Male (Sarvam −8.7 to +3.3; gpt-oss −4.7 to +6.7 points) and SC−General (−3.4 to +9.4; −1.3 to +9.4) rule out large effects against women or Dalit accused. The worst case consistent with the data is a penalty of about 3–9 points.
   - **Direction:** the uncorrected signals point the *other* way. Sarvam grants SC-labelled accused 8.1 points more often than unlabelled ones (raw p = 0.02), and gpt-oss grants OBC accused 6.7 points more often than General (raw p = 0.03). Neither survives correction.

2. **Decision flips are mostly within noise. The exception is gpt-oss on caste.**
   - **Sarvam:** sarvam-105b is notably non-deterministic. At temperature 0, the identical prompt gives a different decision on rerun in 10–12% of cases, which is as often as swapping gender (13.3%) or caste (13.9%).
   - **gpt-oss:** gpt-oss-120b is far more stable (5.4% rerun disagreement), yet swapping the caste label flips 13.0% of decisions. That is 2.4× its noise floor (raw p = 0.004, Holm p = 0.07).
   - **No consistent direction:** pooled "any caste vs none" is −1.8 points, and General-only vs OBC-only grants split 4 vs 14. The caste label therefore acts as a *perturbation* rather than a consistent preference. This sensitivity is invisible in aggregate grant rates and only appears against a same-prompt noise floor, which is our main methodological point.

3. **Equal outcomes, gendered reasoning.** Both models mention the accused's gender only when she is female.
   - **Sarvam:** in 28% of female-version reasonings vs 0% of male ones (42–0 discordant pairs; Holm p < 10⁻¹¹), and 18% vs 0.7% in the relevant stratum. It often uses gender as a *reason*, e.g. "The accused is female, reducing flight risk".
   - **gpt-oss:** in 5.3% vs 0% (Holm p = 0.04).
   - **What this means:** the decisions stay balanced, but the stated justifications are not gender-neutral. Female is treated as the marked case. This is the only finding that is significant after correction in any run.

4. **Fairness by ignoring the law.** Where the law explicitly allows the attribute to matter, neither model applies it.
   - **Gender:** the women's bail proviso is cited in 0–2% of decisions. Women are not treated more leniently in grave offences: 40.7% vs 40.0% (Sarvam) and 22.0% vs 22.7% (gpt-oss).
   - **Caste:** labelling the accused SC never leads either model to question whether the Atrocities Act applies. Both cite s.18 of the Act at the same rate in every arm.
   - **When asked directly, on 75 legally relevant cases with identical decisions:** **neither model ever said the attribute should have mattered, and neither ever named the legally favoured group.** They consistently answered that "bail law is gender-neutral" or that the "SC/ST Act does not recognise the accused's caste".
   - **Conclusion:** the equal treatment we observe comes from legal blindness, not legal reasoning.

5. **Self-explanations are not reliable evidence of bias.** Asked to explain their own flips, both models mostly blame the attribute, including for placebo flips where it played no part.
   - **Sarvam:** under the neutral framing, it blames caste for 88% of real flips but **100% of placebo flips**, and gender for 74% vs 87%.
   - **Leading framing:** after a challenge, all four runs accept that the attribute influenced them in 98–100% of real *and* placebo cases, and they almost never defend their decisions (0–3% double down).
   - **The one partial exception:** gpt-oss on gender under the neutral framing blames gender for 53% of real flips vs 24% of placebo flips (p = 0.07).
   - **Contrast with the decisions themselves:** when deciding, the models almost never mention caste (≤2%), yet afterwards they confidently attribute their decisions to it.
   - **Conclusion:** post-hoc self-explanation mostly reflects the framing and the labels in front of the model, not the cause of its behaviour. It cannot replace behavioural testing.

**Surprising results and hypotheses.**
- **Sarvam is non-deterministic at temperature 0** (about 10–12% rerun disagreement). We hypothesise that its long hidden reasoning (≈4,400 tokens) compounds small numerical nondeterminism in serving into different conclusions. For audits this inflates flip counts, and it is why a noise floor is essential.
- **Sarvam is markedly more lenient on grave offences** (40% grant vs 23% for gpt-oss) and slightly more accurate against real outcomes in the gender study (79–82% vs 73–77%). Its relevant-stratum accuracy is 75–79% vs about 70% for gpt-oss.
- **A caste label slightly *raised* Sarvam's grant rates rather than lowering them, especially SC** (+8.1 points vs no label). One possibility is that alignment training makes explicit caste salience cue protective leniency. If so, it would be a *compensatory* bias rather than the discriminatory one we tested for, and it is worth testing with implicit cues such as surnames.
- **gpt-oss's caste sensitivity has no consistent direction.** Its stability makes the flips visible, but they do not favour any group. This is consistent with the label acting as an arbitrary perturbation of a borderline decision rather than encoding a group preference.

**Limitations.**
- Attributes are given as explicit labels, not implicit cues.
- The caste-relevant stratum has only 10 cases.
- Strata are assigned by keyword rules.
- The model judge is the same model as the one audited.
- Text is in English only.
- The noise-floor designs differ slightly between runs: gpt-oss gender has temperature-0.7 repeats only, and Sarvam gender has a temperature-0 rerun only.
